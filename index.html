Final Statement

Philosophy of Instructional Technology

Brandon Hughes · College of Education, The University of Alabama · November 2026

Introduction

Instructional technology is not the tools. It is the practice of designing learning with technology, and the reasoning behind why a particular tool was chosen for a certain purpose. It is specifically because that tool improves the learning process rather than merely accompanying it. When I wrote my initial personal theory, I could describe what I do in a classroom but not consistently explain why it worked. This statement is the result of putting that reasoning in order. My central claim is that no single learning theory governs good instruction or is necessarily better than others. The right theory depends on the task in front of the learner, and the designer’s job is to match them so the chosen technology meaningfully improves the process.

A Theory Built on Task Fit

Ertmer and Newby (2013) argue that behaviorism, cognitivism, and constructivism are less competing accounts of what learning is than tools suited to different levels of task. Behaviorist design fits recall-level content. Cognitivist design fits procedures and problem solving. Constructivist design fits ill-defined problems where transfer is the goal. A learner memorizing terminology and a learner diagnosing an unfamiliar failure are not doing the same cognitive work, and designing both the same way fails one of them.

That principle is what changed in my thinking. My initial theory stated that I chose strategies based on the objective and where the class sits, which was accurate but amounted to instinct. Task fit gives me a position I can defend and, more usefully, one I can be wrong about. It makes design decisions checkable against something other than my memory of last semester.

Table 1. Task fit, applied
Task in front of the learner Theory that fits Design move that follows Evidence I look for
Retain terminology Behaviorist Short, low-stakes retrieval distributed across the unit Accurate recall after a delay, not immediately after review
Execute a procedure Cognitivist Worked examples first, support reduced as competence grows The procedure completed without prompting
Diagnose an unfamiliar failure Constructivist Virtualized environment where building and breaking carries no cost Transfer to a scenario the learner has not seen
Reason with others Sociocultural / collaborative Peer mediation with the instructor inside the discussion rather than above it Reasoning made visible and taken up by others

A workable theory also has to account for who is doing the learning, not only what is being learned. Sociocultural theory supplies the part my original statement described but never named. Wood, Bruner, and Ross (1976) defined scaffolding as the support that lets a learner complete a task they could not complete alone, withdrawn as competence grows. Van de Pol, Volman, and Beishuizen (2010) sharpened that into three properties that separate scaffolding from ordinary helping: contingency, meaning the support responds to what the learner actually does; fading, meaning the support decreases over time; and transfer of responsibility, meaning the learner progressively takes the task over. I had used a model-then-release sequence for years without those terms. Having them supplies a test. Support only counts as scaffolding if the next attempt receives less of it than the last one did, and if the reduction answers to the learner rather than to the calendar.

Learners and Teachers

Learners in technical subjects arrive with uneven prior experience, and that variance drives nearly every design decision I make. What they tend to share is a desire for autonomy within structure. Ryan and Deci (2000) tie that directly to motivation, arguing that learners invest more when they experience competence, relatedness, and a meaningful degree of self-determination. Technology extends that autonomy by giving learners control over pace, sequence, and the route they take to a common outcome.

That control carries a cost. Greater learner control demands self-regulation that many learners have not yet developed, including goal setting, self-monitoring, and reflection (Zimmerman, 2002). Learners also take on responsibilities with no clear analog in a traditional setting, such as evaluating source credibility, using generative artificial intelligence with academic integrity, and managing a digital footprint.

The teacher’s role shifts correspondingly. Information is abundant and freely available, so the value a teacher adds is not delivery. It is designing the environment, sequencing content, calibrating difficulty so learners remain in productive struggle rather than frustration or boredom, and coaching the judgment calls that technology places in front of them. Doing that well is not a technical skill. Mishra and Koehler (2006) locate it at the intersection of technological, pedagogical, and content knowledge, and the intersection is where the decision actually lives. Knowing a tool and knowing a subject do not add up to knowing which tool serves a particular learning goal.

Instructional Strategies and the Role of Technology

My strategies follow from task fit. New or complex content receives more structure at the outset, because directing novices into open-ended discovery consumes working memory with the search for a solution rather than the structure of one (Sweller, 1988). Once learners have a foundation, support is reduced and the work moves toward independent problem solving.

Low-stakes retrieval is the other constant. I use short ungraded checks and review activities heavily, and I previously explained their value by arguing that low stakes make learners willing to be wrong so the correction sticks. That explanation locates the mechanism in the feedback. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) found that the act of retrieval itself improves long-term retention relative to spending equivalent time restudying, whether or not feedback follows. The correction matters for design. If retrieval is doing the work, then distributing retrieval across a unit matters more than concentrating review immediately before an assessment.

Technology earns its place when it does something instruction cannot do as well without it. Simulation and virtualized environments allow learners to build, break, and repair systems without risk to equipment or to their grades, which lowers the cost of experimentation and reduces the extraneous load that has nothing to do with the objective (Sweller, 1988). Asynchronous resources allow learners to revisit an explanation rather than catch it once.

I previously justified technology mostly by what it removes, such as cost, risk, and scheduling limits. Sociocultural theory frames tools differently, as instruments that change the thinking they support rather than neutral delivery mechanisms. A simulation that records every step a learner took is not simply a cheaper laboratory bench. It makes reasoning visible, which puts that reasoning where a teacher or a peer can respond to it. For collaborative and online work, Harasim (2017) describes Online Collaborative Learning as progressing through idea generating, idea organizing, and intellectual convergence, with the instructor participating as a member of the knowledge community rather than as its source. That is a more accurate account of what I want from online discussion than the convenience argument I originally offered.

Evidence of Learning

Learning is evident when a learner can use something in a situation they have not seen before, without being walked through it. A selected-response score indicates whether terminology was retained. Watching someone work an unfamiliar failure indicates whether they understand.

Method follows purpose. Recall-level content can be checked with brief low-stakes items. Skills require performance. Most of what I collect is formative, because feedback a learner can still act on accomplishes more than feedback delivered after the work is finished (Black & Wiliam, 1998). Technology widens the evidence available. Simulations record the sequence of actions a learner took, screen recordings let learners narrate their reasoning aloud, and digital portfolios collect work into something with value beyond the gradebook.

Ethics, Equity, Accessibility, and Learner Variability

Technology advances equity when it removes barriers and deepens inequity when it assumes resources learners do not have. The Universal Design for Learning framework treats variability as the expected condition rather than an exception and asks designers to build multiple means of engagement, representation, and action and expression from the outset rather than retrofitting accommodations afterward (CAST, 2024). The current version of the guidelines goes further by naming bias in design as something to be made explicit rather than assumed away.

In practice this means designing on the assumption of inequity rather than hoping against it. Nothing essential should depend on home internet access, personal hardware, or a paid subscription. It also means watching for subtler advantages, such as vocabulary that assumes prior exposure, or collaborative work that allows an experienced learner to carry a task while a novice observes. Rotating roles and requiring individual documentation surfaces who is actually doing the work.

Accessibility is a design property rather than a feature appended at the end. Contrast ratios, keyboard operability, and whether feedback is announced to a screen reader are decisions made during construction, and they are testable rather than matters of taste. Ethical obligations extend to vetting tools for privacy before placing them in front of learners, teaching learners to evaluate source credibility, and setting expectations for generative artificial intelligence that distinguish using a tool to think from using one to avoid thinking.

Conclusion

Technology should be used when it does something instruction cannot do as well without it. If a tool does not clear that bar, then other tools will do instead. What has changed in my thinking is not the practice so much as the reasoning underneath it. It has however helped me refine my practice. I became a teacher and was barely able to describe what I do or be able to report that it worked. I can now do that. I can also explain why it works, name the conditions under which it would stop working, and test a design against something more durable than instinct.

Portfolio Narrative

From Instinct to Argument

What the artifacts demonstrate about my learning, my professional growth, and the places where my philosophy actually moved rather than simply acquiring citations.

When this course started I could run a classroom and could not explain it. I knew which strategies worked because I had watched them work, and that is a real kind of knowledge. It is not one I could defend to someone who asked why, and it is not one I could troubleshoot when it stopped working. This portfolio tracks the distance between those two positions.

The three artifacts below are not illustrations of a philosophy I already held. Two of them produced the revisions that are now in it.

The arc, in three stages

  1. Practice without vocabulary

    My original philosophy statement described a model-then-release sequence, heavy use of low-stakes review, and virtualized labs where students build and break systems. All of it accurate. None of it named. I was describing scaffolding, retrieval practice, and cognitive load management without those terms, which meant I could repeat what I did but could not reason about it. Behaviorism and constructivism were present in my practice and absent from my citations.

  2. Naming the mechanisms

    Modules 3 through 5 supplied the vocabulary and, more importantly, the tests that come attached to it. Ertmer and Newby (2013) gave me task fit, which turned “I pick strategies based on the objective” into a position with stated conditions. Van de Pol et al. (2010) gave me contingency, fading, and transfer of responsibility, which turned “I model, then release” into something I can measurably fail at.

    A named mechanism is not a label. It is a claim that can be wrong, and that is the whole value of it. Support only counts as scaffolding if the next attempt receives less of it than the last one did. I can now check that.

  3. Testing the position against feedback

    This is the part I did not anticipate. The Module 3 artifact exposed a gap in my own reasoning that I had carried for years, and the correction is now written into my final philosophy. The chain is documented below.

The short version: I had been massing review immediately before assessments and calling it good practice, because my explanation for why low-stakes review worked located the mechanism in the feedback rather than in the retrieval. Instructor feedback on the Module 3 artifact pointed out that the guide front-loaded all sixteen fallacies before any practice, and that mixing earlier items into each new set would put retrieval to work against that front-loading. Following that back to Roediger and Karpicke (2006) showed me the explanation I had been using was in the wrong place. If retrieval itself is doing the work, the scheduling implication is different from the one I had been following. That sentence is now in my philosophy, and it changes what I will do in a unit plan.

The honest limits

A portfolio that only reports growth is a sales document. Three things in here are not finished.

  • The Module 3 revision is specified but not built. I know what the staged release and mixed practice sets should look like. The published resource still front-loads.
  • The artifacts have been checked for contrast and keyboard operability with automated tools and manual tabbing. None has been tested with a hardware screen reader, which is the test that actually counts.
  • Task fit is a position I can state and defend. I have not yet run a full semester designed deliberately around it, so I cannot report what it looks like when it fails.

Naming those is not modesty. My own philosophy argues that a usable theory has to name the conditions under which it would stop working, and the same standard applies to the portfolio that presents it.

Module 3 · Learning and Instructional Theories

The Pretext Field Guide

Source remixed
Lumen Learning, Common Logical Fallacies (English Composition I), CC BY 4.0
Remix license
CC BY 4.0
Governing theory
Cognitivism (Mayer, 2002); cognitive load (Sweller, 1988)
Audience
Secondary learners studying social engineering

A remix that keeps all sixteen logical fallacies and their definitions from the open source, then rewrites every example as a phishing email, a pretext phone call, or a text scam. The fallacies are grouped by the appeal they exploit, logos, pathos, and ethos. Ten practice scenarios follow: the learner reads a message and names the technique, and the exact manipulative sentence is highlighted with an explanation of why it works. A builder at the end asks the learner to write one.

What this demonstrates about my learning

It demonstrates that I can derive a design decision from a theory claim rather than from taste. Grouping sixteen items into three appeal categories is not decoration, it is chunking, and the choice came from Mayer’s account of managing limited working memory. I could state the reason before I built it.

It also demonstrates the limit of designing from a single theory, which is the more useful finding. The guide presents all sixteen before any practice. That is a coherent cognitivist sequencing decision and it is not supported by what the retrieval literature says about spacing. I did not see the gap until instructor feedback named it, and following that feedback produced the one substantive revision to my final philosophy. An artifact that exposes a flaw in my reasoning is better evidence of learning than one that does not.

Module 4 · Design Process and Integration Frameworks

One Inbox, Three Lenses

Type
Original comparative resource built from assigned course readings
Hosting
GitHub Pages
Frameworks applied
TPACK, SAMR, and Universal Design for Learning
Case held constant
A single phishing-simulation lesson

One lesson, read three ways. Rather than defining TPACK, SAMR, and UDL in sequence, the resource holds a single phishing-simulation lesson constant and applies each framework to it, so the differences between the frameworks appear in what each one notices and what each one ignores. It closes with a comparison and a defended choice.

What this demonstrates about my learning

Two things, one conceptual and one practical.

The conceptual one is a correction. I went in treating SAMR as a hierarchy, with redefinition as the goal and substitution as a failure state. Applying it to an actual lesson showed that the level names the kind of change, not its quality, and that a substitution-level choice can be the correct one when the task does not call for more. That is task fit arriving by a different road, and it is why the Module 4 contribution shows up in my philosophy as the argument about where the real decision lives.

The practical one is structural. The artifact had to be rebuilt mid-course when a fourth framework was removed from the assignment requirements. Because the design held the case constant and varied only the lens, removing a lens left the case, the structure, and the comparison intact. Organizing around the learning problem rather than around the frameworks is what made the artifact survive a changed requirement.

Module 5 · Technology Adoption Models

The Adoption Brief

Remix license

Diffusion of Innovations explained for mentors supporting transitioning veterans as they adopt unfamiliar technology. The brief remixes a general open textbook summary into an audience-specific tool with four interactive components: a stage finder, an attribute explorer, an adopter curve, and a responsible-use checklist. It cites four recent peer-reviewed sources alongside the seminal ones, and includes annotated further reading. The worked example is a composite; no real individual is described.

What this demonstrates about my learning

It demonstrates sociocultural theory functioning as a design constraint rather than as a reading I could summarize. The brief positions the mentor as a More Knowledgeable Other whose standing comes from having done the thing rather than from a title, and it builds support that fades as the adopter moves through the decision stages. That is contingency and fading in Van de Pol’s sense, applied deliberately, and applied outside a classroom to adult learners. Being able to transfer a construct to a context the readings did not cover is the test I would apply to a student, so it is the one I should apply here.

It also forced the equity argument in my philosophy to become concrete. The intended audience includes adults with a wide range of access needs, so the UDL decisions were not optional enhancements layered on at the end. Designing for variability from the outset, rather than retrofitting, is a claim I had written down before this artifact and had not previously had to honor under a real constraint.

Accessibility & Use

What was done to make this site usable, what was done to protect other people, where the material came from, and where generative artificial intelligence contributed.

Accessibility statement

Accessibility here is treated as a design property rather than a feature added at the end, which is the position argued in my philosophy. These are the decisions made during construction, stated so they can be checked.

  • Structure. Semantic landmarks for header, navigation, main, and footer. One level-one heading per view, with heading levels in order and no levels skipped.
  • Keyboard. Every interactive element is reachable and operable by keyboard, with a visible focus indicator that meets contrast requirements. A skip link to main content is the first focusable element on the page.
  • Navigation state. The current page is marked with aria-current so it is announced, not only shown in color. When the view changes, focus moves to the new heading and the change is announced through a polite live region.
  • Contrast. Body text, headings, and interface text meet or exceed WCAG 2.1 AA, which is 4.5 to 1 for normal text and 3 to 1 for large text. Color is never the only carrier of meaning.
  • Figures. Both diagrams are inline SVG with role="img" and a linked title and description, so the full content of each figure is available as text rather than as an image with a short label.
  • Tables. Data tables use real table markup with captions, column headers, and row headers scoped so a screen reader can announce the cell context.
  • Links. Link text describes its destination. External links are marked as opening in a new tab within the link text itself.
  • Motion and sizing. The prefers-reduced-motion setting is honored. The layout reflows to 320 pixels and text resizes to 200 percent without loss of content or function. Touch targets meet the 44 pixel minimum.
  • Media. No audio or video is used on this site, so captions and transcripts are not applicable here. No decorative images are used; both figures are informative and described in full.

Known limitation. This site and the three linked resources were tested by keyboard and with automated contrast checking. None has yet been tested with a hardware screen reader. That test is outstanding and is named as such rather than implied to be complete.

Privacy

  • No student names, student work, student images, or student data appear anywhere on this site or in the linked resources.
  • Course peers are not named, and peer feedback is described in general terms rather than reproduced or attributed.
  • The worked example in the Module 5 resource is a composite. No real individual is described, and no organization is identified or branded.
  • No personal contact information, institutional account details, or third-party analytics are collected or embedded. This site sets no cookies and stores nothing in the browser.
